7  Projected Housing Needs

7.1 How many more people: population to 2050

Fauquier is undoubtedly growing, even when its growth has been stable since 2000. Fauquier’s population is projected to grow from 74,577 residents in 20241 to 93,171 by 2050, an increase of about 18,594 residents. This 24.9% increase in population drives every downstream housing-need estimate in this chapter.

1 The base population is the ACS 2020–2024 5-year estimate, which runs modestly below the PEP July-2024 point-in-time estimate (~75,865) used in Chapter 2; see Appendix A for the reconciliation.

Growth is steady across each decade, from 77,588 in 2030 to 93,171 in 2050. This is a 20.1% gain over twenty years, outpacing Virginia’s projected 14.2% statewide growth until 2050. Warrenton is projected to grow in parallel, from 10,693 residents in 2030 to 12,841 in 2050: up 2,148 residents (also a 20.1% increase).

WarningData note: Bealeton

Bealeton has no separate Weldon Cooper projection and is carried through the county totals.

7.2 How many more households

Using the average-household-size method, or the number of occupied housing units multiplied by the average household size, the county grows from 26,720 households in 2024 to 33,515 by 2050. This is about 6,795 net new households or a 25.4% increase in households.

Compared with constant-headship sensitivity, which measures projected population outcomes based on the proportion of people in a given demographic group that are a head of household, average household size nearly coincides with an estimate of 33,382 households by 2050 (or 6,662 additional households). Only using headship could over or under estimate the projected growth, because of an increase in young adult headship or conversely more multigenerational families.

WarningData note: choice of method

This is a difference of only about 133 households, so the choice of method does not materially change the total. This 6,795 increased figure is the denominator for the production need (Figure 7.3) and the tenure-by-income allocation (Table 7.1) that follow.

7.3 Can production keep pace?

Based on these estimates, the county needs about 269 new units per year to house the projected growth by 2050, plus at least a 3% vacancy allowance. On the numbers alone, the county’s recent production roughly keeps pace with projected need, essentially level with the 266 units per year the county permitted over 2020–2025.

The baseline need is lighter in upcoming years, with only about 204 units/year required through 2030, rising toward the recent pace as growth compounds. The build pressure, or need to add units, is in near-term and more specific to the type and price of what is built, not the overall raw count.

This inverts the Greater Piedmont REALTOR’s study’s finding that units needed (307/yr) exceeds the current building pace. The difference in calculations is relative to the household-size assumptions made. This study uses the county’s actual 2020–2024 ACS average household size of 2.78, whereas the GP study’s +7,737-household figure back-implies a roughly 2.7-person household.

WarningWhy household size matters

A larger average person size spreads the same population over fewer new households, ultimately lowering the annual unit need; the full reconciliation is documented in the Data & Methodology appendix (Appendix A).

Keeping pace with the baseline count is admirable but not the same as meeting need, though. The affordability gaps (Figure 5.4, Figure 5.5) and interviews show the recent product mix has skewed towards the top of the market. The forward challenge is in composition that can meet the diverse needs of those described in Table 7.1.

7.4 How much does the answer swing? One assumption sets the range

The estimate for the baseline need for units is only as firm as its household-size assumption. Holding the county’s actual 2.78-person household size average constant yields about 269 units needed per year, whereas letting size drift down to 2.60 people per household pushes the baseline units needed to 361 units. Therefore, a plausible reading of the same population projection spans roughly 269–361 units per year.

269 units per year is a conservative floor of that estimate range, not its midpoint, sitting essentially on the 2020–2025 permit pace of 266 units/yr.2

2 The Greater Piedmont study’s 307 units/yr estimate falls inside the range, back-implying about a 2.7-person household.

The floor is the locally-grounded case: Fauquier’s own household size has not fallen, measuring about 2.76 in 2010 and 2.76 in 2020 (decennial), and 2.78 in 2024 (ACS). This household size is flat-to-rising, bucking the national downward drift. The 361/yr high end of this estimate range, therefore, is not an extrapolation of local history. It is a sensitivity that assumes the county begins converging toward the smaller households as otherwise seen statewide (Virginia averages 2.52).

7.5 From new arrivals to full need: a scope ladder

How widely “need” is defined changes the units needed. Policy is what can ensure housing unit types developed meet the needs of Fauquier’s new arrivals. Read strictly, the county needs 6,795 units to house projected household growth or about 261/yr. Adding a 3% vacancy allowance lifts that to 6,999 or 269/yr until 2050.

Layering in today’s affordability backlog adds roughly 3,598 units that are needed. This is calculated with a data-grounded 1,059-unit rental deficit estimated for below 80% AMI households (Figure 5.4), plus a conservative, assumption-driven estimate for 2,539 ownership units. The estimated ownership share needed is about one-third of the 7,616 units estimated for the sub-80%-AMI ownership shortfall (the raw for-sale snapshot is not a build target). Adding in these estimates raises the need to 10,597 units built or 408/yr.

WarningReading the ladder

The two rightmost rungs are determined partly on stated assumptions and should be read as scope, not precision.

The county’s stock is aging — median year built 1987, with about 56% built before 1990, so some replacement demand is real even though its size is assumed. To replace obsolete stock involves an assumption since permits capture additions, but not demolitions. At a conservative 0.25%/yr of the 28,621-unit stock, adds about 1,860 units in replaced/updated housing stock.

Altogether, the full-scope total comes near 12,457 units or 479/yr to add supply, add additional under 80% AMI units, and replace low quality/ high maintenance stock.

The single “units needed” number is a definitional choice, not a fact. Housing only new arrivals points to needing about 269/yr. Also clearing the existing shortfall for lower income households - the vast majority of the county - and replacing obsolete stock roughly doubles that.

Critically, the headline ~7,000-unit need already approaches — and the full-scope 12,457 far exceeds — the county’s existing by-right service-district build-out capacity; see Chapter 8 for the policy implications.

7.6 An aging county: the 65+ surge

The 65-and-older population climbs from 17,157 in 2030 to 18,246 in 2040, then holds essentially flat at 18,208 in 2050, as the leading edge of the baby-boom cohort ages through. The surge is concentrated in the next 15 years.

The 75-and-older group grows faster and deeper, from 7,371 to 9,939 before easing to 9,844. The higher-need and older-senior share of all seniors rises from about 43% to 54% over the next 20 years. This is the forward-looking counterpart to the current-state senior profile in Chapter 6 (65+ already 17% of residents, most owning their homes). The demand signal is for accessible, lower-maintenance, and smaller units, where aging-in-place options and downsizing product in the current stock largely lacks.

7.7 Where the need lands: tenure and income

Following the county’s current tenure and income mix, the projected 6,795 new households are split roughly between 5,267 owners (78%) to 1,528 renters (22%). The single largest block is owner households above 100% AMI or 3,566 units - who are likely already served by current permitting and development plans.

Forward growth is not the binding constraint at the bottom — the existing gap is. In the lowest owner and renter income bands, the current affordable-unit deficit (from Chapter 5) dwarfs the projected new need. Even building for all projected growth would leave the existing shortfall for under 80% AMI households untouched.

Because the allocation holds today’s income distribution constant, it describes where demand will arise, not where it is affordable. Read alongside the current gaps (Figure 5.4, Figure 5.5), it frames the forward task as delivering units at price points the recent market (Chapter 3) has not.

For those that said, “Aging in place and downsizing seniors and returning young people have no options,” the county must add about 269 units per year to keep pace with household formation. By addressing seniors’ needs, with accessible and low maintenance housing options, young folks returning will be competing for the same units. Without addressing this gap in options across the household spectrum, the supply in the county will continue to increase but only widens the housing and affordability gap.

TipTown spotlight — Warrenton grows with the county; Bealeton is folded into the county total

Weldon Cooper projects the Town of Warrenton to grow from 10,693 residents in 2030 to 12,841 by 2050 (+2,148, 20.1%), a pace close to the county’s own 20.1% over 2030–2050 (Figure 7.1). As a major water-and-sewer service area within the county’s eight service districts, Warrenton is where much of the projected household growth can physically be absorbed. Bealeton is not projected separately — Weldon Cooper publishes totals only for counties and large incorporated towns, and Bealeton is an unincorporated census-designated place — so its share of growth is carried inside the Fauquier County totals throughout this chapter.
