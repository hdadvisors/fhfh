4  The Rental Market

4.1 Who rents

Fauquier is overwhelmingly an ownership county: only about 21% of households rent. For context, 33% of Virginia’s households are renters. Realtors, employers, and local government interviewees highlighted this, as well. Folks come to Fauquier to buy, and there is a limited rental market.

Warrenton is the exception: roughly 37% of the town’s 3,985 households rent, nearly double the county rate. Warrenton represents the county’s principal rental submarket. Bealeton, on the other hand, tracks with the county at about 21% of its households renting.

WarningReliability

Bealeton’s renter count is small and carries Medium reliability, so it is read as a count, not a precise rate.

4.2 What renters pay

The county’s median gross rent, meaning all housing costs including lease rent and utilities, rose from $1,138 in 2013 to $1,613 in 2024, adding 42% in nominal, and +10% in real terms. Renters have lost ground even after inflation.

In spring 2026, an interviewee shared that, “Only about 2 of 19 available rentals were priced under $1,700.” CoStar professional asking rents, near $1,604 (adjusted), sit at or just above the ACS median. This is consistent with a small stock of newer market-rate apartments pricing above the broader rental pool. This report uses the ACS median gross rent as its affordability benchmark (Chapter 5), since it covers the full rental universe, while CoStar tracks only professionally-managed apartment communities.

Town rents track the county: Warrenton and Bealeton median 2024 rent (High reliability) sits roughly within $50 to $200 of the county figure.

4.3 Where the rental stock prices

The modal rent band in the county is $1,500–1,999 and represents about 25% of county cash-rent renters. Combined with the bands above it, most renters now pay $1,500 or more. Under $1,000 monthly rentals are scarce, representing only about 14% of the county’s renters, underscoring how little of the stock is affordable to lower-income households.

In 2025, the MLS recorded that only 28 of 203 closed leases were under $1,700, about 14%. These data identify a median county lease of $2,450 monthly, consistent with the Greater Piedmont REALTOR’s March 2026 study. This leaves a somewhat larger affordable share than the single snapshot of available rentals suggested, but the story is the same: sub-$1,700 rentals are scarce, and most of the rental market prices well above that (Figure 4.3, Figure 4.2). The MLS median runs well above the county’s ACS median gross rent ($1,613) because MLS only captures leases placed through a broker, which skew toward larger single-family homes and townhouses (the bulk of this study’s rental stock) rather than the smaller apartment units that lease in-house and never reach MLS.

WarningData note: place-level distributions

Place-level distributions are noisy; only the higher-count Warrenton bands survive the reliability screen, and they mirror the county’s upward concentration.

4.4 The professional apartment market

The professional rental market is tiny throughout the county. Roughly 41 multifamily properties appear in the broader CoStar inventory and there are about 998 market-rate units across 14 CoStar-tracked apartment properties. Asking rent in this professional apartment market reached roughly $1,603 per unit in nominal terms (latest quarter). In constant 2025 dollars, asking rents are roughly flat, also near $1,604. Professional operators have kept pace with, not outrun, inflation in the local rental market.

Fauquier has a persistently tight rental market with little slack. Vacancy for these apartments is very low, recently about 2.8%, and under ~3% for most of the past decade.

4.5 Rental stock by structure type

Single-family homes are the backbone of the county’s rental market, about 68% of all renter-occupied units (attached and detached). Single-family detached alone is roughly 57% of the rental market.

Purpose-built apartments (5+ units) are only about 20% of rentals, the mirror image of Figure 4.4’s thin professional market. This could be why so much of the rental supply behaves like the for-sale market: the reliance on scattered single-family rentals means renters compete for the same limited housing stock as buyers.1

1 Place-level structure detail is too small to size reliably, so this is shown county-wide.

4.6 Fair Market Rents vs. actual rents

Because Fauquier is folded into the high-cost Washington, D.C. metro FMR area, the county’s two bedroom (2BR) FMR at $2,246 sits far above the actual ACS median 2BR rent of $1,437. This is a gap of roughly $800.

Small Area FMRs (SAFMRs) pull the benchmark towards what locals actually experience, described as a high cost and scarce rental environment. SAFMRs still land above the ACS median rent at every bedroom size, with the Warrenton and Bealeton 2BR SAFMR ranges from $1,520 to $2,340, with Bealeton (22712) at the lowest.

The practical implication in the differences in FMR and ACS rents is ultimately favorable for voucher holders, with generous payment standards, something an interviewee pointed out, as well. However, this means FMRs overstate the going asking rental market and can be a poor gauge of local affordability. The ACS medians (Figure 4.2) are the better yardstick for rooting rents into local reality.

4.7 Assisted inventory & preservation risk

Fauquier’s assisted housing rental stock consists of 874 units across 13 federally supported properties. This is a small cushion against the market rents documented above. The program mix locally is dominated by Low-Income Housing Tax Credit deals, with a handful of older project-based Section 8, PRAC/811 (elderly/disabled project rental assistance), and USDA Rural Housing (515) properties whose subsidies are the closest to expiring.

Rental affordability preservation at these properties is a real risk and near-term, with the soonest subsidy expiration in January 2027. Additionally, 8 of the 13 properties have a subsidy expiring by 2036, the window in which preservation decisions must be made. As assistance units are aging, the quality of these rentals throughout the county is likely lower comparatively to the other aging single family rentals.

TipTown spotlight — assisted units concentrate in and around Warrenton

Most of the county’s assisted properties sit in Warrenton (and nearby Remington), the same submarket that carries the county’s highest renter share (Figure 4.1). Bealeton has little to no dedicated assisted inventory in the NHPD record, so the affordable options there are effectively limited to whatever scattered single-family and market-rate units price low, which Figure 4.3 shows is a shrinking share.
