5  Affordability Gaps

5.1 What sets the standard: Area Median Income

For affordability, it is typical to use the “Area Median Income” (AMI) benchmark. Fauquier is part of the Washington, D.C. metro income-limit area, whose published median family income is $166,100 annually. This metric is far above what local wages (Figure 5.6) would suggest.

WarningHUD’s 80% income cap

HUD caps the 80% income limit at the national median, so in the D.C. metro the 80% limit sits lower than its usual spacing from the 50% limit would suggest. The large jump from 80% to 100% AMI is an artifact of that cap, not a data error.

In other words, for a 4-person household, 80% AMI is $106,800 and 100% AMI is $166,100. These are the reference points behind the affordability thresholds used throughout this chapter. Additionally, these are the income benchmarks that housing programs throughout Fauquier must use.

5.2 What each income band can afford

For context, a 50% AMI household can only afford about $1,869 in housing costs, which falls within the modal rent band between $1,500 and $1,999 and is slightly above the ACS median gross rent of $1,613. It is below the $2,450 median MLS closed lease, which skews toward larger broker-listed homes. This household could buy a home of roughly $149,261, a fraction of the $650,000 median sale price, and widely unavailable throughout the county.

With questions about quality surrounding the age of apartments throughout the county, as well as limited availability of units, finding a rental with space for a family that does not exceed $1,869 would be a challenge.

Greater affordability rises steeply with increased household income: only at 100–120% AMI does the affordable purchase price clear a quarter-million dollars. Even an affordable purchase price for a household at 120% AMI is $497,149, short of the county median sales price at $650,000.

These thresholds, and not local wages, define who the market can and cannot serve; the gaps that follow measure the distance between them.

WarningA note on “median rent”

This chapter anchors on the ACS median gross rent ($1,613) — it covers the full universe of occupied rentals and carries the highest reliability of any rent figure in this study (Chapter 4). Two other figures appear elsewhere and mean something different: CoStar’s professionally-managed asking rent ($1,604) tracks only market-rate apartment communities, and the MLS closed-lease median ($2,450) reflects broker-side leases, which skew toward larger single-family homes and townhouses rather than the smaller units that make up most of the rental stock — not a market-wide asking rent.

5.3 Who bears the cost: households by income and tenure

The divide of housing tenure, renting vs. owning, is ultimately a division of income. Low-income households skew heavily toward renting. Renters are a large share of all households in both the county’s 30% and below and 30-50% AMI bands. Owners dominate in the above 80% AMI bands across the county, Warrenton, and Bealeton.

Overall, Warrenton carries a higher renter share across every income band, consistent with its role as the county’s principal rental submarket. Bealeton’s cells are small and are read as counts, not precise rates.

WarningData note: CHAS universe

CHAS detail sums to roughly 25,900 occupied households county-wide. This is the denominator behind the burden and gap figures that follow.

5.4 Cost burden over time (ACS)

WarningDefinition: cost burden

Cost burden is defined as paying more than 30% of household income towards housing costs. This is an important and widely tracked metric, as it can help identify both reasonable payment benchmarks across income bands and help to identify vulnerabilities in the housing market.

Renters in Fauquier are consistently the more cost burdened group: about 40% of renters were cost-burdened in 2024 versus 21% of owners. This is a gap of nearly 20 points that has held across the decade.

Luckily, burden has edged down recently, not up. Since 2013 renter cost burden has moved from 44% down to 40%, while owner cost burden has moved from 29% to 21% over the same time period. This could reflect income growth and refinancing opportunities during the low-rate decade. That said, these burden levels remain high, especially for renters, and the recent rate environment increases for owners (Chapter 3) points in the opposite direction for overall relief.

Both towns run hotter than the county on renter cost-burden: 55% of Warrenton’s renters and almost 59% of Bealeton’s renters are cost burdened. These data sit well above the county line (2024) and affirm interview suspicions surrounding rental affordability.

TipTown spotlight — Warrenton and Bealeton carry the county’s highest renter burden

Both towns run hotter than the county on renter cost burden versus 40% countywide. Warrenton’s cell is High reliability, while Bealeton’s is Medium (CV 15–30%) and is read as a count-based signal, not a precise rate. As the county’s principal rental submarket (Figure 5.1), Warrenton concentrates both the renters and the burden — the same submarket where the affordable-rental deficit (Figure 5.4) bites hardest.

5.5 The core picture: burden is concentrated at the lowest incomes (CHAS)

Overall, renters are more cost burdened than owners within every income band, and severe burden is especially concentrated among the lowest-income renters: the households with the least disposable income to absorb a rent increase or utility cost change.

Burden collapses onto the bottom of the income ladder. At lower than 30% AMI, the vast majority of both renters and owners are cost-burdened. Most of them are severely cost burdened, meaning these households pay half or more of their income towards housing costs. Conversely, above 80% AMI the cost burden all but disappears for renters.

WarningData note: CHAS vs. ACS burden

Measured on the CHAS universe, county burden is 33% for renters and 20% for owners, lower than the ACS headline (Figure 5.2) because CHAS uses a different denominator; the two are reconciled in the Data & Methodology appendix (Appendix A).

5.6 The rental gap: too few homes for the lowest incomes

The deepest shortfall in rentals is among the lowest income bands. The county has about 625 fewer rentals that are affordable and available to renter households with less than 30% AMI. These are the households with essentially no market alternative.

All income bands run a deficit of rental units for a net shortfall of roughly 3,315 units across the county. As a result, higher-income renters occupy units that would otherwise be affordable to lower-income households, a competition-down-the-ladder dynamic, reflecting renter realities shared in interviews. Those renting need to be quick and involved in the word of mouth market to get a decent apartment.

The gap is a supply problem, not a targeting problem: even where units are nominally affordable, they are already occupied with higher income households. This leaves the lowest-income renters with the fewest options.

5.7 The ownership gap: almost nothing for sale below 100% AMI

Below the 100% AMI price ceiling, there is almost nothing for sale: just 8 of 218 active listings, effectively excluding low to moderate-income buyers. For lower income bands, 0, 0, 1, and 7 active listings are affordable across the 30%, 50%, 80%, and 100% AMI bands. Realtors interviewed stated that they cannot take on clients seeking homes for $250,000 and less, because they know these households will not find success in Fauquier.

The overwhelming majority of listings, 210 of 218, are priced only within reach of the 120% AMI band or above, mirroring the median-price gap in Table 5.2. Owner-household counts far exceed affordable listings in every income band below the highest wealth households, so first-time and lower-income buyers face a market with essentially no entry point. Sales are ultimately most successful when seeking out higher priced properties.

5.8 The bottom line: wages don’t reach the cost of housing

The all-jobs average wage ($68,000 annually) narrowly covers the county’s median rent ($64,520 needed), a margin of about $3,480, or roughly 5%. That headline flips, though, once you look sector by sector: Health Care ($60,062), Local Government ($58,079), and especially Retail Trade ($41,934 yearly) all pay below the $64,520 needed. This could lead to greater cost burden for households working in Retail Trade or even the Local Government, or contribute to in and out migration and daily commuting patterns for local workers.

As interviewees reminded us frequently, “Workers cannot afford to both work and live here.” Several interview participants shared that they cannot even afford to live in Fauquier despite working there. The data bear this out most clearly for buying and for the lower-wage sectors: even a two-earner household of two average local jobs ($136,000) falls well short of the $190,586 needed to buy the median priced home. Ownership, not rental, is where the wage-to-cost gap is structural and unavoidable.

The message is ultimately structural and systemic: the county’s wage base and its housing costs have decoupled. Dual-income working households are priced out of the ownership market, while the lowest-wage sectors are priced out of the market entirely - both rentals and ownership opportunities. The wage to housing cost gap is not just a perception, despite it being a consistent theme throughout interviews. This gap of wages and housing costs has become a defining structural feature of Fauquier’s housing market.
