Executive summary

Fauquier County has a highly priced housing market with a moderate working income environment, where the majority of residents commute away for work and those working within the county must commute in. The county is housing affluent households that commute elsewhere for high paying work, but it sustains short term and lower wage employment where workers must live elsewhere.

Key findings

  • Housing choices are very limited. Single-family detached homes are 85.1% of Fauquier’s housing stock (Virginia: 61.3%), and the 266 permits/year issued over 2020–2025 were almost entirely single-family — 2–4 unit “missing middle” production is effectively zero across the full 2000–2025 record (1  Current Inventory and Production Trends, Figure 1.2, Figure 1.5).

  • Fauquier is a bedroom community with a lower-wage local job base. 76.4% of employed residents (26,762 of 35,028) commute out of the county, and the average local job pays $68,000 — a county median household income of $130,189 built on wages earned elsewhere (2  Demographic Shifts & Socioeconomic Factors, Figure 2.10, Figure 2.9).

  • The entry-level for-sale market has disappeared. The median sale price reached $650,000 in 2025, and just 3 of 218 active listings were priced under $350,000 — while monthly payments on the median home rose +152% since 2016 against a +81% price increase and +43% income growth (3  The For-Sale Market, Figure 3.4, Figure 3.3).

  • Rentals are mostly houses, and the subsidized stock is thin and aging. Single-family homes carry ~68% of all renter-occupied units and purpose-built apartments only ~20%; the county’s entire assisted inventory is 874 units across 13 properties, 8 of which have a subsidy expiring by 2036 (4  The Rental Market, Figure 4.5, Table 4.1).

  • Housing cost burden is concentrated at lower incomes. 40% of renters are cost-burdened versus 21% of owners. Every AMI band runs a rental deficit for a net shortfall of ~3,315 units, and only 8 of 218 for-sale listings are affordable below 100% AMI — buying the median home requires $190,586, about $60,000 above the actual median income (5  Affordability Gaps, Figure 5.4, Figure 5.5, Figure 5.6).

  • Older adults are the most exposed group already in place. 32% of the county’s 9,173 senior households (2,922) are older adults living alone, and 41% of elderly renter households are cost-burdened — against a rental market with little dedicated senior product (6  Vulnerable Populations, Figure 6.1, Figure 6.3).

  • Production keeps pace with growth but not with need. The county is projected to add 6,795 households by 2050, requiring ~269 units/year — essentially level with the 266/year recent pace — but adding today’s affordability backlog and replacement of aging stock lifts full-scope need to 12,457 units (7  Projected Housing Needs, Figure 7.3, Figure 7.5).

  • Meeting that need is not possible under current zoning. Remaining by-right capacity inside the eight service districts is plausibly 4,000–4,500 units today (from an 8,381-unit 2010 Comprehensive Plan baseline, net of ~15 years of permitting) — below even the ~7,000-unit headline need, forcing a choice among expanding service districts, increasing density, accepting unmet need, or absorbing rural spillover (8  Conclusions).
