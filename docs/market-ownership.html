3  The For-Sale Market

3.1 Sales volume

Sales peaked at 1,466 sold in 2021 and have since fallen to 938 in 2025 (down about 36% from the pandemic-era high). The cooling reflects Fauquier’s thin supply and higher financing costs, not falling demand, as prices kept climbing through the slowdown (Figure 3.2).

Counts through 2021 come from the GP/VAR annual series and 2022 onward from Bright MLS; 2026 is a partial year-to-date count and is not comparable to the full years.

WarningA note on sales counts

Bright MLS’s published monthly summary reports roughly 20% more sales than the transaction-level export used for the volume series above (e.g. 1,127 vs 938 closings in 2025) — a near-constant level offset across every year, consistent with a property-type filter on the export. Median sale prices agree between the two sources to within ~0.1%, so the price findings are unaffected; the level of sales counts (and the active-listing snapshot below) should be read as the export’s narrower definition, while the year-over-year trend holds under either source.

3.2 Prices

The county’s median sold price nearly doubled in nominal terms: about $360k in 2016 to $650k in 2025, an 81% increase. Even after inflation, real prices rose from roughly $465k to $650k in 2025 dollars, a 40% increase in genuine appreciation, not just inflation. The town ZIPs track the county: Warrenton (~$690k in 2025) runs above Bealeton (~$570k).1

1 Town-ZIP series begin in 2022 because the county’s 2016–2021 monthly series is VAR (county-only). These are ZIP-code cuts (20186/20187 for Warrenton, 22712 for Bealeton), not town boundaries — the Warrenton ZIPs in particular extend well beyond the town limits.

3.3 Prices vs. payments vs. incomes

Over 2016–2025 the median home sales price rose +81%, but the monthly payment on that home rose +152 percent. This is likely a reflection of the 30-year rate that climbed from 2.96% in 2021 to 6.60% in 2025, compounding the price increase. Median household income rose only +43% through 2024, far behind both. Buying power has fallen sharply and this is widely recognized amongst residents and employers alike.2

2 The pattern matches the GP study’s Figure 9 direction (GP: price +79%, payment +127%, income +35%); FHFH’s actuals run somewhat higher on all three but tell the same story — payments ≫ prices ≫ incomes.

3.4 Price segments

In a focus group for realtors, participants shared the unit types missing from Fauquier’s market: starter and downsize homes (often the same unit). Homes under $250k fell from 3.8% of sales in 2022 to 1.6% in 2025. Everything under $350k is now barely 6% of the market. As of the July 2026 listing export, just 3 of 218 active listings were priced under $350,000.

The $750k+ home price band has surged to 35% of sales from 21% in 2022. The modal band is now $500–750k (or 41% of sales).

This is the tail end of a long collapse in the starter home market: the Greater Piedmont REALTOR’s identified that under-$250k sales fell from 27.8% in 2016 to 3.0% in 2021. The MLS-only view here (2022+) shows the effective erosion of the market for entry level homes.

3.5 Days on market & supply

Despite the recognition, as well as data confirmation, of the gap in availability for starter style homes, the housing market (with larger single family homes) is still hot. Homes sell fast with a median 10 to 11 days on the market in 2022 - 2024. This rose to 21 days on the market in 2025, reflecting a cooling effect from increased rates. This is still a matter of weeks, not months.

The monthly view exposes a strong seasonal rhythm that the annual averages can hide. Listings linger on the market for roughly 25 to 40 days in December through February and sell in under 10 days by spring yearly.

Active for-sale inventory has climbed from a tight 315 listings a month in 2022 to 392 in 2025. There’s a real loosening of the pandemic era squeeze with months of supply on the market - 3.4 to 4.3 months.

Even so, supply stays well below the roughly six-month mark that separates a seller’s from a buyer’s market: Fauquier remains a seller’s market, just a less extreme one than at the 2021–22 peak.

TipInventory is recovering, not recovered

Contrary to a point-in-time reading, the monthly Bright MLS series shows active listings and months of supply trending upward since 2022 (from ~3.4 to ~4.3 months) as higher rates slowed the frantic pace. Fauquier is still firmly a seller’s market, with supply short of the ~6-month balance point, and homes selling within weeks for most of the year.

3.6 What it takes to buy

Homes available for sale in Fauquier are expensive relative to what local incomes can support. That said, both housing prices and incomes have risen in 10 years. However, affording the $650k median home requires about $190,586 in household income under standard underwriting, versus Fauquier’s actual $130,189 median, a gap of roughly $60,000. The gap is driven by the same forces as outlined in Figure 3.3: high prices and a 6.49% mortgage rate. The full owner/renter gap by income band is developed in Chapter 5.

The squeeze is amplified by financing: monthly payments rose far faster than prices or incomes (Figure 3.3). “Buyers face a quality-versus-price mismatch — what’s affordable isn’t desirable, and what’s desirable isn’t affordable.”

This is more nuanced than interview participants realize: as new construction skews larger and pricier (Chapter 1), the sub-$350k segment has all but disappeared (Figure 3.4), and the market still moves fast (Figure 3.5). This leaves entry-level buyers with few options at any quality level.
